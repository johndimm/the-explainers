Shylock, a moneylender, is offering Antonio a loan. "Take no doit of usance for my moneys" means he's initially offering the loan without interest ("usance" refers to interest on a loan, and a "doit" is a small coin). He's trying to appear generous, hoping to overcome Antonio's past insults and secure his friendship, a contrast to his later vengeful actions.

The scene is fraught. Shylock, the Jew, offers Antonio, a Christian merchant, a loan—an act of apparent generosity cloaked in something far colder. "Take no doit of usance for my moneys" means he's initially waiving interest—the fee typically charged for borrowing money. This isn't altruism; it's a calculated move, a performance of magnanimity designed to disarm Antonio's suspicion, perhaps even to ingratiate himself. A "doit" is a small coin, the very suggestion of a waived interest payment highlighting the peculiar nature of the transaction. The underlying tension, however, is palpable. This isn't about friendship; it's the calculated calm before the storm of a meticulously planned pound of flesh. Shylock's offer, a seeming act of kindness amidst simmering resentment, ultimately prefigures the contract's brutal terms, a carefully constructed narrative masking the true cruelty to come. The whole exchange feels like a performance—a very carefully staged performance of civility.
